The project crossed a threshold during the trench episode. Before it, this was a script that draws a plot; after it, it is a pipeline that can defend its numbers - parity anchoring, source cross-checks (IV_ref), filters with counted drops, and documented failure modes. That property is worth more than any feature. If this is portfolio or thesis material, the most valuable artifact you own is the trench story itself: symptom, falsified hypothesis (q made it worse), model-free diagnostic, structural fix, regression test. Write it up as a case study; it demonstrates exactly the skill that distinguishes quantitative work from plotting.
What I would add, in priority order:

Anchor live mode too. The strongest cheap improvement. Live yfinance spot and chains are ~15 minutes delayed and not captured in the same instant - the same desync class, smaller magnitude, currently unguarded. anchor_to_forward is source-agnostic and already in the codebase; applying it to live chains removes the q guessing there as well and makes both modes methodologically identical. One conditional in app.py.
A pytest file. Every fix in this conversation was verified by a synthetic regression test that now exists only in our chat: price-to-IV roundtrip, the 2% desync recovery, crossed-quote dropping, pagination under a row cap, the CSV cache roundtrip. Frozen into tests/, they cost nothing and protect every future refactor. A pipeline whose correctness was hard-won and is not regression-tested will silently lose it.
No-arbitrage diagnostics on the rendered surface. Two checks turn the viewer into an instrument: calendar (total variance sigma squared times T must be non-decreasing in T at fixed forward moneyness) and butterfly (convexity of price in strike). Violations flagged in the log panel distinguish "the market is strange today" from "my inputs are broken" - the question you will otherwise ask yourself for every odd-looking date.
Per-date rates. Now the largest remaining systematic error for deep history. The same DoltHub author publishes a rates database, so the plumbing pattern already exists in your codebase. Anchoring shrank the damage (F is measured, not derived), so this matters mainly for long-dated options in the 2020-2021 era - which is exactly the history most interesting to scrub.
SVI smile fitting, if you want a research direction. griddata is honest but dumb; the standard next step is fitting a parametrization (SVI) per expiry and rendering the fitted surface. You gain smoothness with meaning - five interpretable parameters per maturity whose time series you can study - and arbitrage checks become analytic. This changes the project from visualization to modeling; only take it if that is the intent.

Warnings, in order of probability of biting you:
yfinance is the fragile leg now. It scrapes unofficial Yahoo endpoints that break several times a year, and it carries your trading calendar, live mode, and historical spots. When it breaks, the symptom will look like your bug. Check yfinance's GitHub issues before debugging your own code.
DoltHub's database is one hobbyist's free service. No SLA, no guarantee of tomorrow. Your accumulated data_dolt/ CSVs are the durable asset - back that directory up; it is irreplaceable in a way the code is not.
Do not treat the surface as tradeable truth. EOD mids of retail-grade data, European model on American options, constant r: the shape and its evolution are sound for study; absolute levels in the wings and deep history are approximations. The honest claim this project supports is "the market's volatility expectations, reconstructed defensibly" - not "fair value."
What I would not add: intraday data (your sources cannot supply it; faking it with the old scraper reintroduces the desync class), a database (the CSVs are the right weight), more UI (the app does its job; effort spent there now displaces effort on the model), and American pricing engines (a legitimate fork, but it changes the project's identity - finish this one first).
